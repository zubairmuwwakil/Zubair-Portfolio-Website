I had a clear picture of where I'd be by now.

Working at a big tech company. A portfolio worth something. A couple of properties. Married, maybe a kid. I was specific about it, the way you are when you're young enough to think a plan is a schedule.

Then a serious car accident interrupted my degree at the University of Toronto, and none of that happened on time.

I'm not going to write about the accident. It's not the interesting part, and it isn't what I want anyone to take from this. The interesting part is what happens to you afterward, when the plan you built your sense of yourself around stops being available.

What happened to me is that I felt like I was losing.

## The part nobody puts on their profile

I didn't want to be a failure.

That's the honest version. Not "I was motivated by resilience" or any of the other things you're supposed to say. I felt like I was falling behind a future I'd already decided was mine, and watching the gap widen while other people kept moving.

There were opportunities I badly wanted that didn't come through. Not one. Several, over a few years, including some I thought I had. They stack up in a way that's hard to explain to people who haven't had a run of them. Each one on its own is fine. Twenty of them and you start to wonder whether the problem is you.

Some days it felt like swimming toward the surface with a ball and chain on my ankle. I could see where I wanted to be. I just couldn't get there at the speed I'd promised myself.

I'm writing that down because I think a lot of people are somewhere similar right now and almost nobody says it out loud. The internet is full of people whose careers look like a straight line. Mine doesn't. I suspect most don't.

## Why I kept building anyway

Two things are true at once, and I've stopped trying to pick one.

Part of it was fear. I didn't want to look back and find out I'd had more in me than I used. That's not a noble reason but it's a real one, and it got me to the desk on days nothing else would have.

But the other part is simpler. I love building software.

I can disappear into a project for hours, days, weeks, and not get bored. I like learning something I don't understand yet. I like the moment an idea stops being a thought and starts being a thing that runs.

And I love that you never know where it goes. You start with one problem, find five more along the way, and the thing you end up with is not the thing you set out to make. It branches. It changes direction. Sometimes it becomes something you couldn't have pictured at the start.

When the plan fell apart, building was the one place I could still move forward. So I kept going.

## It started with pickleball

I was coaching at a school in Courtice.

What I kept noticing was how much time the coaches spent organizing instead of coaching. Writing out schedules by hand. Working out matchups. Moving people between courts. Trying to keep everyone playing without anyone sitting too long.

And I remember thinking: why are we still doing all of this by hand?

It bothered me because that time belonged to the players. They came to learn and have fun, and the people running it were spending their energy on paperwork instead.

So I built something. It started as Excel tools and a few automations. Then I hit the limits of what a spreadsheet could do, found more things I wanted to fix, and kept going.

That became The Pickleball Social. It's on the App Store now, across 29 releases, and it runs sessions for clubs with over a thousand players between them.

I never started with a vision for a platform. I started with one problem I thought I could solve, and the rest came from following it.

## The moment I actually understood what I was doing

There's one thing I'll probably always remember.

Someone close to me organized weekly matches. She put real effort into getting everyone together and making sure people had somewhere to play.

And one day I saw her on the brink of tears, because it had become too much.

People were cancelling. Replacements had to be found. Schedules had to be redone. All of it landed on her, every week, and almost none of it was noticed by the people it was for.

That's what got me. She was doing it for everyone else, and the work was invisible.

So I built group management. Players take responsibility for their own availability. Replacements get found without her chasing anyone. The person organizing stops carrying the whole thing alone.

I remember showing it to her for the first time. She said, "Wow, you built that just for me?"

That meant a lot. But it wasn't the part that stayed with me.

About a week later, she was different. Less stressed. Smiling more. Actually playing pickleball again instead of spending the whole session managing everyone else's game. Every time she saw me she had this huge grin.

That was when I understood what the work is for.

It wasn't about how clever the code was or how impressive the architecture sounded. It was someone I cared about being genuinely happier because of something I made.

That's the kind of impact I want my work to have. Everything I've built since has been measured against that.

## Why I keep ending up in money

The other thing I keep coming back to is finance, and it took me a while to notice the pattern.

I've worked in banking and mortgage underwriting. I've built reporting automation for a finance team. And on my own time I keep making tools about money. PickMe, for getting more out of the credit cards you already have. Looply, for not losing money to return windows and subscriptions you forgot about. In Unity, for seeing the whole picture.

They look like different projects. They come from the same place.

Most people are never properly taught how money works. Not credit, not investing, not how a small decision now turns into a large one later. And that's unfair, because money is the thing that either holds you back or sets you free.

I care about that personally. Financial freedom is one of my own goals, and I'm not going to pretend otherwise. But I also like teaching people, and I've seen from the inside how confusing these systems are for the people using them and the people working behind the counter.

So I build tools that make the knowledge usable. It's the same instinct as the pickleball thing. Someone is carrying something they shouldn't have to carry alone.

## Where I actually am

I went back to school. I'm finishing the computer science degree at Ontario Tech, graduating April 2028, while working and shipping.

That sentence took longer to earn than it looks.

And I want to be honest about the rest of it. I'm proud of what I've built. I also haven't reached most of what I set out to reach, and I find it hard to appreciate the progress because I'm usually thinking about the distance left. Some days staying positive is genuinely tiring. Sometimes I wish it had just worked out the first time.

People say failure builds character. I think that's true, and I also think it's easy to say about someone else's failure. It doesn't make it pleasant while you're in it.

But I'm still here, still learning, still building.

## What I'd want you to take from this

Life rarely goes according to plan, and sometimes it's in those moments when things don't work out that your true character shines.

If you're reading this and you're behind where you thought you'd be, in school or work or money or just generally, I'd want you to see some of yourself in it. I haven't figured it out either. I've had setbacks, missed chances, and plenty of moments where I questioned whether I was good enough.

What I'd ask you is the question I had to ask myself. Not "how do I get back on the original plan," because sometimes that plan is gone. But: how do I make the best of the situation I'm actually in?

Because the thing I love most about building software is that you never really know where an idea will take you. It branches. It changes. It becomes something you couldn't have imagined when you started.

I'm starting to think life works the same way.

Maybe the future doesn't look like what you planned. Maybe you end up somewhere completely different.

That doesn't mean it can't still be something worth having.
